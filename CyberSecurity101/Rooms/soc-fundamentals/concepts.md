# SOC Fundamentals - Concepts

## SOC Tier Structure
SOC teams are typically organized into tiers that reflect increasing levels of expertise and responsibility. Tier 1 Analysts are the first line of defense, monitoring dashboards, triaging alerts, and determining whether alerts represent genuine security incidents or false positives. They follow standard operating procedures (SOPs) and escalate confirmed incidents to Tier 2. Tier 2 Analysts conduct deeper investigations, analyze affected systems, contain threats, and determine the scope and impact of incidents. They also perform proactive threat hunting to identify hidden threats. Tier 3 Analysts are senior experts who handle complex incidents, perform advanced malware reverse engineering, develop custom detection rules, and mentor junior staff. This tier structure ensures efficient use of resources, with each level handling tasks appropriate to their skills.

## Alert Triage
Alert triage is the process of evaluating incoming alerts to determine their validity, severity, and required response. The triage process includes: verification (confirming the alert is not a false positive), classification (determining the type of threat), prioritization (assigning severity based on potential impact), and assignment (routing to the appropriate handler). Triage decisions rely on contextual information: the affected assets (are they critical?), the nature of the activity (is it known malicious or merely anomalous?), the user involved (is this normal behavior for them?), and the current threat landscape. Effective triage reduces alert fatigue by filtering out noise and ensuring analysts focus on genuine threats.

## SOC Metrics and KPIs
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) measure SOC effectiveness and efficiency. Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) measures how quickly the SOC identifies security incidents after they occur. Mean Time to Respond (MTTR) measures how quickly the SOC contains and remediates incidents. Alert volume and false positive rate indicate the quality of detection rules. Escalation rate measures how many alerts require higher-tier analysis. Ticket aging measures how long incidents remain open. These metrics help SOC managers identify bottlenecks, justify resource allocation, and demonstrate SOC value to organizational leadership. However, metrics should be interpreted carefully to avoid incentivizing behaviors that reduce security effectiveness.

## SIEM Correlation and Alerting
A SIEM ingests log data from diverse sources (firewalls, servers, endpoints, applications), normalizes it into a common format, and applies correlation rules to identify patterns indicative of security threats. Correlation rules can be simple (a single event from one source) or complex (multiple events across different systems within a time window). For example, a correlation rule might alert when an account has 10 failed login attempts within 5 minutes (brute-force detection), or when a user logs in from two geographically impossible locations within 30 minutes (impossible travel detection). SIEM alerts are sent to analysts through dashboards, email, or integration with ticketing systems.

## Threat Intelligence Integration
Threat intelligence provides context that transforms raw alerts into actionable insights. Tactical intelligence (IoCs such as IP addresses, domains, file hashes) enables automated blocking and alert enrichment. Operational intelligence provides details about attacker tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs). Strategic intelligence informs long-term security planning. SOCs typically subscribe to multiple threat intelligence feeds (commercial, open-source, industry ISACs) and integrate them into SIEM and EDR tools. When an alert fires, enrichment pulls relevant threat intelligence to provide context about the indicators involved, helping analysts make faster, better-informed triage decisions.

## SOAR and Automation
Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response (SOAR) platforms automate repetitive SOC tasks through predefined playbooks. For example, a phishing playbook might automatically: extract indicators from a reported email, check them against threat intelligence, query the SIEM for related activity, isolate the affected endpoint, and create a ticket with all gathered information. Automation reduces analyst workload, ensures consistent response procedures, and accelerates containment actions. SOAR platforms also orchestrate workflows across multiple security tools, enabling coordinated responses that would be difficult to execute manually in real-time.
