# Incident Response Fundamentals - Concepts

## NIST Incident Response Lifecycle
The NIST SP 800-61 framework defines four phases of incident response. Preparation involves creating policies, assembling teams, acquiring tools, and conducting training before any incident occurs. Detection and Analysis involves identifying potential incidents through monitoring, alerting, and reporting, then confirming and characterizing the incident. Containment, Eradication, and Recovery involves stopping the incident from causing further damage, removing the root cause, and restoring normal operations. Post-Incident Activity involves analyzing what happened, updating plans and controls, and documenting lessons learned. The lifecycle is not strictly linear; some phases may repeat as new information emerges during the response.

## Indicators of Compromise (IoCs)
IoCs are pieces of evidence that indicate a system may have been compromised. Atomic IoCs are discrete, immutable pieces of data: IP addresses, domain names, email addresses, URLs, and file names. These can be used for automated blocking and detection but are easily changed by attackers. Computed IoCs are cryptographic hash values of files (MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256) that uniquely identify known malware samples. Behavioral IoCs describe patterns of activity: unusual login times, impossible travel (logins from geographically distant locations within short timeframes), large data transfers, unusual network protocols on standard ports, and deviations from baseline process execution. Behavioral IoCs are more difficult for attackers to evade but require more sophisticated detection methods.

## Incident Classification and Severity
Incidents are classified by type (malware infection, phishing, unauthorized access, denial of service, data breach, insider threat) and assigned severity levels based on potential impact. Critical severity indicates active data exfiltration, compromise of critical systems, or ongoing attacks that threaten human safety. High severity indicates confirmed compromise of sensitive data or critical systems. Medium severity indicates confirmed incidents with limited scope or potential incidents requiring investigation. Low severity indicates suspicious activity that does not meet incident criteria but warrants monitoring. Severity determines response speed, resource allocation, and notification requirements. Incidents can be reclassified as new information becomes available.

## Containment Strategies
Containment prevents an incident from causing additional damage. Short-term containment focuses on immediate threat neutralization: isolating a host from the network (disconnecting the cable, disabling the network interface), disabling compromised user accounts, blocking attacker IP addresses at the firewall, or shutting down affected services. Long-term containment implements temporary fixes while permanent solutions are developed: applying temporary access controls, implementing rate limiting, deploying web application firewall rules, or creating network segmentation. Containment decisions involve trade-offs: aggressive containment may alert attackers or destroy evidence, while slow containment increases damage. The incident commander must balance these factors based on the specific situation.

## Eradication and Recovery
Eradication removes all traces of the attacker from the environment: deleting malware, removing persistence mechanisms (registry Run keys, scheduled tasks, services, startup items), revoking attacker access, patching exploited vulnerabilities, and resetting compromised credentials. Eradication must be thorough; missing a single backdoor can lead to reinfection. Recovery restores normal operations: restoring systems from clean backups, rebuilding compromised servers from scratch, restoring data from verified backups, and gradually returning systems to production with enhanced monitoring. Verification steps include vulnerability scanning, integrity checking, and monitoring for recurrence of attack patterns before declaring the incident resolved.

## Post-Incident Analysis
The post-incident phase is critical for organizational learning. A lessons learned meeting brings together all stakeholders involved in the response to discuss what went well, what could be improved, and what actions should be taken to prevent similar incidents. The incident report documents the complete timeline, actions taken, evidence collected, root cause analysis, and financial impact. Findings from post-incident analysis should drive improvements to detection rules, response procedures, security controls, and employee training. Organizations that skip this phase repeat the same mistakes, while those that invest in continuous improvement mature their incident response capabilities over time.
