# General Cybersecurity Interview Questions & Answers

## 1. Explain the CIA Triad.

**Answer:** The CIA Triad is the foundation of information security: 1) Confidentiality - ensuring data is accessible only to authorized parties (encryption, access controls, least privilege). 2) Integrity - ensuring data is accurate and not modified by unauthorized parties (hashing, digital signatures, version control, checksums). 3) Availability - ensuring systems and data are accessible when needed (redundancy, backups, failover, DDoS protection, SLA). Beyond CIA: Authentication (verifying identity), Authorization (access rights), Non-repudiation (can't deny actions - logs, digital signatures), Accountability (audit trails). The Parkerian Hexad adds Possession/Control, Authenticity, and Utility.

## 2. What is defense in depth?

**Answer:** Defense in depth is a layered security strategy where multiple controls provide redundancy - if one fails, others continue protection. Layers: 1) Physical security (locks, badges, biometrics, guards). 2) Network security (firewalls, IDS/IPS, segmentation, VPNs, NAC). 3) Endpoint security (AV/EDR, application whitelisting, patch management). 4) Application security (SAST/DAST, WAF, secure coding). 5) Data security (encryption at rest/transit, DLP, classification). 6) Identity/Access (MFA, PAM, SSO, RBAC). 7) Administrative (policies, training, incident response, risk management). Principle: No single control is perfect - layers compensate for each other. Like an onion: peel one layer and there's another underneath.

## 3. Explain risk management concepts.

**Answer:** Risk = Likelihood × Impact. Risk management process: 1) Risk identification - find assets, threats, vulnerabilities. 2) Risk assessment - quantify/qualify risks (qualitative: High/Med/Low; quantitative: ALE = SLE × ARO). 3) Risk response options: Avoid (eliminate activity), Mitigate (reduce likelihood/impact - most common), Transfer (insurance, outsourcing), Accept (acknowledge and monitor). 4) Risk monitoring - continuous review. Frameworks: NIST RMF, ISO 27005, FAIR (quantitative). Key terms: Asset, Threat (potential danger), Vulnerability (weakness), Exploit (how threat uses vulnerability), Threat Vector (attack path), Attack Surface (sum of vulnerabilities).

## 4. What is the difference between a threat, vulnerability, and risk?

**Answer:** A threat is a potential cause of an unwanted incident - something that could cause harm (hacker, malware, natural disaster, insider). A vulnerability is a weakness that could be exploited (unpatched software, weak passwords, misconfiguration). Risk is the potential for loss when a threat exploits a vulnerability. Formula: Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact. Example: A hacker (threat) exploits an unpatched Apache server (vulnerability) using a known exploit, resulting in data breach (impact) - this creates risk for the organization. Threat actors: nation-states, cybercriminals, hacktivists, insiders, script kiddies, APT groups.

## 5. Explain the incident response process.

**Answer:** NIST SP 800-61 defines 4 phases: 1) Preparation - IR team, tools, playbooks, training, communication plan. 2) Detection & Analysis - monitor logs, SIEM alerts, user reports, analyze indicators of compromise (IOCs). 3) Containment, Eradication & Recovery - short-term (isolate host, disable account), long-term (patch, rebuild from clean image), recovery (monitor for reinfection). 4) Post-Incident Activity - lessons learned, improve processes, update playbooks, forensic report. SANS has 6 phases: Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Lessons Learned. Key metrics: MTTR (Mean Time to Respond), MTTD (Mean Time to Detect), dwell time (time from compromise to detection). Communication is critical throughout.

## 6. What are the different types of security controls?

**Answer:** Controls categorized by function: 1) Preventive - stop incidents before they occur (firewalls, encryption, authentication, access controls, policies). 2) Detective - identify incidents in progress or after (IDS, SIEM, log monitoring, CCTV, antivirus). 3) Corrective - fix/recover after incident (backups, patches, system restoration, incident response). 4) Deterrent - discourage attackers (warning banners, guards, visible cameras). 5) Compensating - alternative controls when primary cannot be implemented. Categorized by nature: Physical (locks, guards, fences), Technical/Logical (encryption, firewalls, AV), Administrative (policies, training, background checks). A defense-in-depth strategy uses all control types.

## 7. Explain the principles of least privilege and separation of duties.

**Answer:** Least privilege: users get only the minimum permissions needed to perform their job. Implement via RBAC, time-based access, just-in-time (JIT) privileges. Reduces attack surface - if account compromised, damage is limited. Applies to users, processes, applications, services. Separation of duties: critical tasks require multiple people (no single person has end-to-end control). Example: developer doesn't deploy to production (requires DevOps); one person requests purchase, another approves. Prevents fraud and errors. Dual control: two-person rule for sensitive operations (e.g., key ceremonies). Mandatory vacation forces exposure of fraudulent activities by disrupting day-to-day control.

## 8. What is threat modeling?

**Answer:** Threat modeling identifies and prioritizes threats to a system. Common methodologies: 1) STRIDE (Microsoft) - Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, Elevation of Privilege. 2) PASTA (Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis) - 7-stage risk-centric approach. 3) LINDDUN - for privacy threats. 4) OCTAVE - organizational risk-focused. 5) Attack trees - graphical representation of attack paths. Process: Define scope -> Identify assets -> Create architecture diagram -> Identify threats -> Rate risks -> Mitigate -> Validate. Tools: Microsoft Threat Modeling Tool, OWASP Threat Dragon, Draw.io with STRIDE. Questions: What are we building? What can go wrong? What are we doing about it? Did we handle everything?

## 9. Explain the difference between authentication and authorization.

**Answer:** Authentication verifies identity (proof of who you are) - factors: something you know (password), something you have (token, phone), something you are (biometrics), somewhere you are (geolocation), something you do (behavior). Multi-factor authentication (MFA) uses 2+ factors. Authorization determines what an authenticated entity can access and do - enforced via Access Control Lists (ACLs), Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC), Policy-Based Access Control (PBAC). IAM (Identity and Access Management) combines both. SSO (Single Sign-On) authenticates once, accesses multiple systems. Federation extends authentication across organizations (OAuth, SAML, OpenID Connect).

## 10. What is the difference between symmetric and asymmetric encryption?

**Answer:** Symmetric encryption: same key encrypts and decrypts. Fast, efficient for bulk data. Key exchange problem. Algorithms: AES (standard), ChaCha20, 3DES (deprecated). Key sizes: 128-256 bits. Asymmetric encryption: public/private key pair. Slower, solves key distribution. Used for key exchange, digital signatures. Algorithms: RSA (2048-4096 bit), ECC (256-521 bit), Diffie-Hellman. Use cases: TLS hybrid - asymmetric for handshake/key exchange, symmetric for bulk data. Choosing: AES-256 for data at rest, TLS for transit, ECC for modern performance requirements. Key length equivalence: 256-bit ECC ≈ 3072-bit RSA ≈ 128-bit symmetric.

## 11. Explain common network attacks.

**Answer:** 1) MITM (Man-in-the-Middle) - attacker intercepts communication (ARP spoofing, DNS spoofing, rogue AP). 2) DoS/DDoS - overwhelm service (SYN flood, UDP amplification, HTTP flood). 3) DNS attacks - cache poisoning, tunneling, hijacking. 4) Session hijacking - steal session cookies/tokens. 5) Replay attacks - retransmit captured data. 6) Packet sniffing - capture unencrypted traffic. 7) Port scanning - discover open ports/services. 8) Social engineering - phishing, pretexting, baiting. 9) Supply chain attacks - compromise software before delivery (SolarWinds). 10) Zero-day exploits - unknown vulnerabilities. 11) Ransomware - encrypt data, demand payment. 12) Insider threats - malicious or negligent employees.

## 12. What is social engineering and what are common techniques?

**Answer:** Social engineering exploits human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities. Techniques: 1) Phishing - fraudulent emails appearing legitimate (spear phishing targets specific individuals, whaling targets executives). 2) Smishing - SMS phishing. 3) Vishing - voice phishing (phone calls). 4) Pretexting - creating fabricated scenario to gain information. 5) Baiting - leaving infected USB drives, offering free downloads. 6) Tailgating - following authorized person through secure door. 7) Quid pro quo - offering service in exchange for information. 8) Impersonation - pretending to be IT support, vendor, or executive. Defense: Security awareness training, policies prohibiting information sharing, verify identity through separate channels, physical security protocols, MFA limits effectiveness of credential theft.

## 13. Explain the NIST Cybersecurity Framework.

**Answer:** NIST CSF provides policy framework for cybersecurity. 5 core functions: 1) Identify - understand risks to systems, assets, data (asset management, risk assessment, governance). 2) Protect - safeguards to limit/deter impact (access control, awareness training, data security, maintenance). 3) Detect - identify incidents quickly (continuous monitoring, detection processes, anomalies/events). 4) Respond - take action on detected incidents (response planning, communications, analysis, mitigation). 5) Recover - restore capabilities, improve (recovery planning, improvements, communications). Implementation tiers: Partial (Tier 1), Risk-Informed (Tier 2), Repeatable (Tier 3), Adaptive (Tier 4). Profiles align framework to business requirements. The CSF is widely adopted across industries, designed to complement existing risk management.

## 14. What is a SIEM and how does it work?

**Answer:** SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) aggregates and analyzes log data from across the organization for security monitoring. Components: 1) Log collection - agents (syslog, WinEventLog, file monitoring) send logs to central collector. 2) Normalization - parse diverse log formats into common schema. 3) Correlation - rules/analytics detect suspicious patterns across sources. 4) Alerting - notify SOC of incidents. 5) Dashboarding - visualization of security posture. 6) Reporting - compliance (PCI DSS, HIPAA, SOX). 7) Retention - long-term log storage for forensics. Key capabilities: real-time monitoring, threat hunting, incident response support, compliance reporting. Major SIEMs: Splunk, ELK Stack (Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana), QRadar, ArcSight, Sentinel (Azure), Securonix. SOAR extends SIEM with automated response.

## 15. Explain the difference between IDS, IPS, and Firewall.

**Answer:** Firewall: Network security device filtering traffic based on rules (allow/deny). Operates at L3-L4 (basic) to L7 (NGFW). Traffic must pass through it (inline). No traffic analysis - just allow/block decisions based on policies. IDS (Intrusion Detection System): Monitors network traffic passively (via SPAN/mirror port or TAP). Analyzes packets for malicious patterns/signatures/anomalies. Alerts on detection but does NOT block traffic. IPS (Intrusion Prevention System): Inline (traffic flows through it). Analyzes and can block malicious traffic in real-time. Active prevention. NGFWs often include IPS functionality. Modern trend: Combine all three (UTM - Unified Threat Management) for small/medium environments, separate appliances for large enterprises.

## 16. What is a vulnerability assessment vs penetration test?

**Answer:** Vulnerability Assessment: Automated scanning to identify known vulnerabilities (CVEs, missing patches, misconfigurations). Tools: Nessus, OpenVAS, Qualys, Nexpose. Output: prioritized list of vulnerabilities. Frequency: weekly/monthly/quarterly. Broader coverage, less depth. Can generate false positives. Penetration Test: Manual, goal-oriented attack simulation. Tests exploitability of vulnerabilities, chain exploits, test business logic, attempt to reach specific objectives (domain admin, data access). Types: Black box (no info), White box (full info), Gray box (partial). Deeper but narrower. False positives verified manually. Often has rules of engagement and scope. Compliance may require both: VA for continuous coverage, pentest annually or after major changes.

## 17. Explain the concept of zero trust architecture.

**Answer:** Zero Trust: "Never trust, always verify." No implicit trust based on network location. Every access request must be authenticated, authorized, and encrypted. Key principles: 1) Verify explicitly - authenticate and authorize based on all available data (identity, device health, location, behavior). 2) Least privilege access - limit access with JIT/JEA (Just-In-Time/Just-Enough-Access). 3) Assume breach - segment access, encrypt all traffic, continuous monitoring. NIST SP 800-207 defines ZTA components: Policy Decision Point (PDP), Policy Enforcement Point (PEP), continuous diagnostics. Implementation: Micro-segmentation (legacy firewalls at workload level), identity-aware proxies, beyondCorp/Zero Trust Network Access (ZTNA). Not a product but a strategy. Cloud and remote work accelerate ZT adoption.

## 18. What is the difference between GDPR, HIPAA, PCI DSS, and ISO 27001?

**Answer:** GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) - EU regulation for personal data protection. Rights: access, deletion, portability. Fines: up to 4% of global revenue. Applies to any org processing EU citizens' data. HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) - US healthcare data protection. Protected Health Information (PHI). Requires BAA with partners. Fines/tiers based on negligence. PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) - credit card data security. 12 requirements including encryption, access control, testing. Fines from card brands, possible loss of ability to process payments. ISO 27001 - international standard for Information Security Management System (ISMS). Voluntary certification. Requirements: risk assessment, security controls (Annex A), continuous improvement (PDCA). Framework, not prescriptive like PCI DSS.

## 19. Explain the concept of a SOC (Security Operations Center).

**Answer:** A SOC is a centralized team/function monitoring and defending an organization's security posture. Tiers: Tier 1 (Triage) - monitor alerts, initial investigation, escalate incidents. Tier 2 (Analyst) - deep investigation, containment, remediation recommendations. Tier 3 (Expert/Hunter) - advanced threat hunting, reverse malware, forensics, process improvement. Key tools: SIEM, EDR, SOAR, Threat Intelligence Platform, Ticketing System. Processes: Alert triage, incident handling, threat hunting, reporting. SOC models: Internal (full control, costly), MSSP (outsourced), co-managed (hybrid), virtual (distributed). Metrics: MTTD, MTTR, alerts per day handled, false positive rate, escalation rate. Maturity: Level 1 (reactive) to Level 4 (proactive/predictive).

## 20. What is the difference between hashing and encryption?

**Answer:** Hashing: One-way function (irreversible). Fixed output length regardless of input. Deterministic (same input = same hash). Purpose: integrity verification, password storage. No key involved (but HMAC uses key). Collisions possible (two inputs with same hash). Examples: SHA-256, SHA-3, bcrypt (password hashing). Encryption: Two-way (reversible with key). Output length varies with input. Needs key. Purpose: confidentiality of data. Uses: communicating securely, storing secrets. Examples: AES, RSA, ChaCha20. Password storage must use hashing (with salt) not encryption - if encryption key is stolen, all passwords can be decrypted. Hashing is integrity; encryption is confidentiality. Often used together (e.g., TLS encrypts traffic, hashes verify integrity).

## 21. Explain common web application vulnerabilities (simplified overview).

**Answer:** 1) SQL Injection - malicious SQL queries via user input (steal/modify database). 2) XSS (Cross-Site Scripting) - inject scripts into web pages (steal sessions, deface). 3) CSRF (Cross-Site Request Forgery) - force authenticated user to make unintended requests. 4) IDOR - access unauthorized resources by modifying IDs. 5) SSRF - make server request internal systems. 6) File upload vulnerabilities - upload malicious files (web shells). 7) Security misconfiguration - default creds, directory listing, verbose errors. 8) Broken authentication - weak passwords, session hijacking. 9) Cryptographic failures - weak TLS, exposed secrets. 10) RCE (Remote Code Execution) - execute arbitrary commands on server. Defense: Secure coding, input validation, output encoding, authentication controls, regular testing.

## 22. What is a firewall and what types exist?

**Answer:** A firewall monitors and controls network traffic based on security rules. Types: 1) Packet Filter - stateless, examines IP headers (source/dest IP, port, protocol). Fast, limited context. 2) Stateful - tracks connection state (ESTABLISHED, NEW, RELATED), allows return traffic automatically. Standard for most networks. 3) Application/Proxy - inspects application-layer data, terminates connections, provides deep packet inspection. Slower but more secure. 4) Next-Gen (NGFW) - combines firewall + IPS + application awareness + identity-based policies + threat intelligence. 5) WAF (Web Application Firewall) - protects web apps from L7 attacks (SQLi, XSS). Implementation: network-based (hardware/VM), host-based (iptables, Windows Firewall), cloud (Security Groups, NACLs, Cloud Armor). Key features: NAT, VPN, QoS, logging.

## 23. What is the cyber kill chain?

**Answer:** Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain describes stages of a cyberattack: 1) Reconnaissance - gather intel on target (email harvesting, network scanning, social media). 2) Weaponization - create payload (exploit + backdoor bundled in document). 3) Delivery - send weapon to target (phishing email, USB drop, compromised website). 4) Exploitation - trigger exploit (user opens file, vulnerability triggered). 5) Installation - install malware on system (backdoor, RAT, web shell). 6) Command & Control (C2) - establish communication channel to attacker (HTTP, DNS, custom protocol). 7) Actions on Objectives - accomplish goal (data exfiltration, encryption, lateral movement). Defenders aim to break the chain at any stage. Criticisms: linear model doesn't fit all attacks (especially APTs), doesn't cover insider threats well. MITRE ATT&CK provides a more comprehensive framework.

## 24. Explain the difference between blue team and red team.

**Answer:** Red Team: Offensive security team simulating real-world attacks. Goal: identify vulnerabilities, test defenses, think like an adversary. Activities: penetration testing, social engineering, physical security testing. Emulates TTPs of specific threat actors. Often runs as adversarial simulation with specific rules of engagement. Blue Team: Defensive security team protecting the organization. Goal: detect, prevent, and respond to attacks. Activities: monitoring (SOC), incident response, vulnerability management, hardening, threat hunting. Design and implement security controls. Purple Team: Collaboration between red and blue to improve overall security. Red shares findings, blue improves detections/controls. In some orgs, purple is its own team that facilitates communication. The best security programs have both teams working together.

## 25. What is the MITRE ATT&CK framework?

**Answer:** MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge) is a globally-accessible knowledge base of adversary TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, Procedures). It's organized as a matrix: Tactics (columns - "why" - 14 tactics like Initial Access, Execution, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, Defense Evasion, Credential Access, Discovery, Lateral Movement, Collection, Exfiltration, Command and Control) and Techniques (rows - "how" - hundreds like T1059 Command and Scripting Interpreter, T1566 Phishing, T1003 OS Credential Dumping). Sub-techniques detail specific variations. Enterprise covers Windows, macOS, Linux, Cloud, Network; Mobile covers iOS/Android. Use cases: threat intelligence (map adversary behavior), detection engineering (build detections for specific techniques), red teaming (emulate specific groups), gap analysis (which TTPs can you detect?), risk assessment. ATT&CK Navigator visualizes coverage.

## 26. Explain the importance of patch management.

**Answer:** Patch management is the process of acquiring, testing, and applying patches to fix vulnerabilities. Importance: 1) Address known vulnerabilities exploited by attackers (WannaCry used MS17-010 - patch was available months before). 2) Compliance requirement (PCI DSS 6.1, many regulations). 3) Reduce attack surface. Challenges: Testing for compatibility issues, avoiding business disruption (reboots), legacy systems (unsupported), critical systems requiring uptime (OT/ICS). Best practices: Use centralized patch management (WSUS/SCCM for Windows, Landscape/Spacewalk for Linux, tools like ManageEngine, Ivanti). Risk-based prioritization (patch critical CVEs with active exploitation first). Test in staging before production. Have rollback plan. Regular vulnerability scanning to identify missing patches. Zero-day vulnerabilities have no patch - need compensating controls.

## 27. What is business continuity and disaster recovery?

**Answer:** Business Continuity (BC): ability to maintain essential functions during and after a crisis. Broader scope - covers any disruption. Business Continuity Plan (BCP) includes procedures for various scenarios. Disaster Recovery (DR): subset of BC focused on restoring IT infrastructure after a disaster. Key metrics: RTO (Recovery Time Objective) - max acceptable downtime. RPO (Recovery Point Objective) - max acceptable data loss (how recent must data be). Strategies: Backup (tape, disk, cloud), Hot site (fully operational, real-time sync), Warm site (partially equipped), Cold site (empty but ready), Active-Active (all sites in use), Active-Passive (one standby). Testing: tabletop exercises, parallel testing, full-interruption testing. Cloud DR: Backup as a Service, Disaster Recovery as a Service (DRaaS). Phases: Assessment, Planning, Implementation, Testing, Maintenance.
